A Convincing Result Still Needs to Answer the Question

During Booz Allen Hamilton’s Summer Games program, I contributed to an AI team serving NOAA NESDIS. I researched comparable approaches and use cases, explored Ollama and AI queries, participated in testing and benchmarking, refined user stories, and led the structure of the final presentation.

This new exercise makes one part of that reasoning inspectable. Can richer metadata help a person find relevant material when their wording differs from a document’s title?

The Evaluation Decision Publish the query, expected relevant records and ranking together. A reviewer can examine the judgment instead of accepting an unexplained aggregate score.

The baseline matches words in titles. The candidate adds tags and descriptions and normalizes selected terms with a small synonym dictionary. It weights title, tag and description matches differently. Those weights are assumptions for this demonstration.

One Query Exposes the Difference Between Finding and Filtering

For the query “ocean heat readings”, title matching returns no record. Expanded metadata returns the three records below. The authored judgment identifies D01 and D12 as relevant to the intended ocean-temperature need.

Only records with a positive score are returned. If none matches, the output stays empty. Filling the list with arbitrary records would conceal a retrieval failure.

Separate Coverage From the Effort to Find a Useful Result

Across this eight-query fixture, title matching finds a relevant record in the top three for three queries. Expanded metadata does for all eight. That is hit rate at three, 37.5% and 100%, respectively.

Precision asks how much of the first three is useful. Recall asks how much of the judged relevant material was recovered. Reciprocal rank asks how early the first useful result appears. Keeping the query table beside those measures makes their different meanings visible.

Precision retains a denominator of three even when fewer records are returned. Ties use document ID, making repeated runs stable. The evaluator checks unknown relevance IDs, duplicate records and empty-result behavior.

The Result Justifies a Stronger Experiment, Not a Production Claim

The corpus, queries and synonym dictionary were authored together. This deliberately favorable fixture demonstrates evaluation mechanics; it cannot estimate performance on unseen tasks. The candidate also changes two things at once, searchable fields and normalization, preventing attribution to either change alone.

The Next Decision Freeze the methods, collect unseen queries and obtain independent relevance reviews. Compare title matching, added fields and normalization as separate conditions before adding complexity.

A real evaluation would distinguish machine response time from the person’s complete information-finding task. This exercise includes neither a language model nor real user testing and makes no claim about employer query-time savings.
